💰 39 Years Ago Today

On March 13, 1986, a 30-year-old college dropout rang the opening bell — and minted 10,000 millionaires overnight.

His name was Bill Gates. His company was Microsoft. And the IPO that launched that morning at $21 a share would become one of the most lucrative bets in the history of capitalism.

By the end of that single trading day, shares had surged to $35.50. Gates' personal stake — 45% of the company — was worth $350 million before the sun set. He was 30 years old. And this was just the beginning.

📖The Company That Almost Stayed Private

Microsoft didn't want to go public. Bill Gates, famously private and control-obsessed, had watched other tech founders lose influence over their companies after IPOs. He valued control of his time and his company more than personal wealth — and Microsoft's business was already throwing off enormous cash. There was no financial need to go public at all.

But the decision was made for him. SEC regulations required that once a company exceeded 500 private shareholders, it had to go public. Microsoft had crossed that threshold. Gates had no choice.

"Going public is one of capitalism's major sacraments, conferring instant superwealth on a few talented and lucky entrepreneurs."

— Fortune Magazine, 1986

⏱️Microsoft's Rise to the Top
1975Bill Gates (19) and Paul Allen (21) found Microsoft in Albuquerque, New Mexico. First product: a BASIC interpreter for the Altair 8800 home computer kit.
1980IBM comes knocking. Microsoft licenses DOS and becomes the operating system backbone of the PC revolution. The deal that changed everything.
1986March 13: IPO at $21/share. Gates becomes one of the 100 richest Americans at age 30. Microsoft's market cap hits $777 million on day one.
1990Windows 3.0 launches and sells 10 million copies in two years. Microsoft now dominates both the OS and applications markets simultaneously.
1999Microsoft's market cap peaks above $600 billion during the dot-com boom. Gates is the richest person in the world — a title he holds for nearly 20 years.
TodayMicrosoft is one of the world's most valuable companies, now powered by Azure cloud and AI. Every $21 IPO share, accounting for splits, is worth thousands of dollars.
🏆Why a Software Company Changed Everything

Before March 13, 1986, most investors didn't take software companies seriously. Hardware was the real business — you could touch it, ship it, count it. Software was seen as intangible, difficult to value, and easy to copy. Microsoft's IPO shattered that misconception.

The frenzy of that first trading day — shares changing hands multiple times in a single hour — signaled something new. Intellectual property, not physical goods, would define the most valuable companies of the coming century. Amazon. Google. Apple. Meta. All of them owe a debt to March 13, 1986.

And the ripple effect in Seattle alone was extraordinary. Microsoft created so many millionaires among its early employees that it birthed an entire generation of Pacific Northwest philanthropists, venture capitalists, and tech founders. Bezos moved to Seattle partly because of that culture. The Amazon story begins, in part, with a Microsoft IPO.

"Microsoft was not dominated by venture capital investors hungry to harvest their gains. The business gushed cash."

— Fortune Magazine, 1986
