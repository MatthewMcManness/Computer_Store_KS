🌐 36 Years Ago Today

On March 12, 1989, a scientist handed his boss a two-page memo. His boss called it "vague but exciting."

That memo became the World Wide Web. And with it, every computer on Earth — including the one you're reading this on right now — became something entirely different.

His name was Tim Berners-Lee. He was 33 years old, working at CERN — the particle physics laboratory in Geneva, Switzerland. His job was to figure out how to get researchers' computers to talk to each other better. What he ended up inventing changed the entire world.

📄The Memo That Nobody Read

The title was modest almost to the point of invisibility: "Information Management: A Proposal." Berners-Lee circulated it among colleagues on March 12, 1989. His manager, Mike Sendall, scrawled "vague but exciting" across the cover page and filed it away. Nobody said yes. Nobody said no. Berners-Lee was simply told he could work on it on the side, between other projects.

He worked on it for two years. By Christmas 1990, he had written the first web browser, the first web server, and defined three technologies that still underpin every website you visit today: HTML (how pages are structured), HTTP (how they're requested), and the URL (how they're addressed).

"I just had to take the hypertext idea and connect it to the TCP and DNS ideas and — ta-da! — the World Wide Web."

— Tim Berners-Lee
🎁The Gift He Gave the World for Free

In 1993, CERN made the decision that may be the single most consequential act of generosity in history: they released the World Wide Web software completely free, with no royalties, no patents, no licensing fees. Forever.

Berners-Lee himself refused to patent it or profit from it. He has said repeatedly that had the web been proprietary — owned by a corporation — it would likely have fragmented into competing systems, just as the early online world had with CompuServe, AOL, and Prodigy. Instead, it became the single open platform that connected humanity.

⏱️From Memo to World Wide Web
1989March 12: Berners-Lee circulates "Information Management: A Proposal." Manager scrawls "vague but exciting" on the cover. Work begins quietly on the side.
1990By Christmas, Berners-Lee has written the first web browser, first web server, and defined HTML, HTTP, and URL. The web is live — for a handful of CERN researchers.
1991August 6: The web is opened to the public internet. People outside CERN can access it for the first time. There are exactly a few dozen websites.
1993CERN releases the web software royalty-free. The same year, Mosaic — the first graphical browser — launches and the web explodes in popularity.
1994Berners-Lee founds the World Wide Web Consortium (W3C) to oversee web standards. Amazon, Yahoo, and Netscape all launch within the next 12 months.
Today5 billion people use the web. It has generated tens of trillions of dollars in economic value. The memo that nobody read became the infrastructure of civilization.
🏆Why It Still Matters

Every email you've sent. Every photo you've posted. Every video you've streamed. Every order you've placed online. Every job listing you've browsed. Every article you've read. It all flows through the infrastructure that one quiet scientist sketched out in a two-page memo in 1989.

Berners-Lee was later knighted by the Queen of England. He received the Turing Award — computing's Nobel Prize — in 2016. And he still believes the web's best days are ahead of it, even as he warns about the ways it's been twisted away from his original vision of openness and freedom.

Today, 36 years later, you're reading this on a device connected to the web — probably over Wi-Fi, possibly on a computer from a store in Topeka, Kansas. Not bad for a memo nobody read.

"The web does not just connect machines, it connects people."

— Tim Berners-Lee
