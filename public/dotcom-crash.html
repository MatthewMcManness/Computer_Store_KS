💻 25 Years Ago Today

On March 10, 2000, the NASDAQ hit 5,048. Then it all came crashing down.

Twenty-five years ago today, a Friday morning on Wall Street looked like any other. The NASDAQ Composite index — the home of every hot internet stock — had just closed at an all-time high of 5,048.62. Investors were euphoric. The internet was going to change everything. Companies with no revenue, no profits, and sometimes no real product were worth billions. The future, everyone agreed, had already arrived.

By Monday morning, it was over.

🚀How the Bubble Inflated

It started with a genuinely exciting idea: the World Wide Web was going to transform commerce, communication, and daily life. That part turned out to be true. What wasn't true was that every company with ".com" in its name deserved a billion-dollar valuation.

Between 1995 and March 2000, the NASDAQ surged 572% — fueled by venture capital flooding into startups at a dizzying rate, and investors willing to overlook one small detail: almost none of these companies were making any money. The business plan didn't matter. The path to profit didn't matter. What mattered was the story. Get big fast. Dominate the internet. Worry about profits later.

"Most Internet companies continue to be money-burners. For many, there seems to be little realistic hope of profits in the near term."

— Barron's magazine, March 2000 — one week before the peak

Companies spent lavishly on Super Bowl ads, celebrity endorsements, and office perks — all funded by investor cash they hadn't earned. During Super Bowl XXXIV in January 2000, 17 dot-com companies bought airtime, spending roughly $44 million on commercials. Three months later, most of them were gone.

📉The Day the Music Stopped
The peak. Then the freefall.
NASDAQ Composite · March 10, 2000 · 5,048.62 · All-time high

The weekend after the peak, news broke that Japan had entered a recession. It was the pin that pricked the balloon. Monday's trading triggered a global sell-off that hit technology stocks hardest. The NASDAQ suffered one of its biggest single-day point losses in history. Then it kept falling.

By April — just one month after the peak — the NASDAQ had already lost 34% of its value. Mid-April brought its worst week ever, a drop of more than 25% in five trading days, eclipsing even the crash of Black Monday in 1987. The companies that had seemed invincible just weeks earlier were suddenly burning through their last reserves of cash, desperately looking for a lifeline that wasn't coming.

💀The Famous Flameouts
Pets.comRaised $82.5M at IPO in February 2000. Shut down in November 2000 — just nine months later. Market cap went from $300M to zero. The sock puppet mascot lived on as a pop culture cautionary tale.
WebvanOnline grocery delivery. Raised over $800M, built a massive warehouse network across the country, then declared bankruptcy in 2001. Sound familiar? The idea itself was fine — it just came 20 years too early.
Boo.comEuropean online fashion retailer spent $188 million building a global shopping platform before it had a single profitable customer. Liquidated in 2000, six months after launch.
eToys.comAt its peak, eToys was worth more than Toys "R" Us — a company with hundreds of actual stores and decades of real profits. eToys filed for bankruptcy in 2001.
AmazonNot a flameout — but a near-death experience. Amazon's stock fell from $107 to $7 during the crash. Barron's reported they had just 10 months of cash left. They survived by eventually doing the one thing most dot-coms refused to: turning a profit.

"In the month before the NASDAQ peaked, corporate insiders were selling 23 times as many shares as they were buying. Average investors were left holding the bag."

— TED Ideas, revisiting the dot-com crash
🏆What the Crash Taught Us

The internet itself was never the problem. The ideas — online shopping, streaming, digital communication — turned out to be exactly as transformative as the optimists promised. The companies that survived the crash, like Amazon, eBay, and Google, went on to become some of the most valuable businesses in history.

The lesson wasn't that the internet was a fraud. The lesson was that hype is not a business model. A company has to actually do something, serve real customers, and eventually generate real revenue. The ones who understood that survived. The ones chasing the story — the Super Bowl ads, the hockey-stick projections, the "get big fast" fantasy — evaporated.

It took the NASDAQ 15 full years to claw back to its March 2000 peak. Fifteen years. Millions of ordinary investors — people who'd done what they were told, bought and held — watched their savings collapse. Meanwhile, the insiders had already cashed out.

Twenty-five years later, the technology that survived the crash is woven into the fabric of daily life. The hype-driven bubble is a history lesson. And the stores that made it through — the ones that sold real products to real people in real communities — are still here.
